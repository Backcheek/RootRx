The Botanical Compendium

An indexed repository of regional flora, featuring traditional naming systems, precise habitat details, and historical therapeutic applications verified across indigenous frameworks.

Classified Flora Profiles

Ashkibwaan

Jerusalem Artichoke

Helianthus tuberosus L.

Habitat: Moist soils across eastern Canada and northwestern US. Grows 5 to 10 feet tall with rough branching stems and golden-yellow autumn flower heads.

Traditional Uses: The iron-rich, sweet, nut-like edible tubers contain simple carbohydrates that convert into natural sugars without raising glucose levels, making them excellent dietary staples for managing diabetes.

Doodooshaboojiibik

Common Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale

Habitat: Highly ubiquitous perennial found all over North American yards, fields, and roadsides, standing up to 20 inches tall with a hollow, milky stalk.

Traditional Uses: Virtually every part holds value: young leaves are eaten raw in salads, crowns are steamed as hot vegetables, and roasted roots act as caffeine-free coffee. Used to remedy gallbladder problems, urinary disorders, high blood pressure, gout, and acne.

Ginebigo-ojiibik

Seneca Snakeroot

Polygala senega

Habitat: Prefers high-altitude rocky or limestone environments from New England to the Canadian Rockies. Stands 0.5 to 2 feet tall with white/pink spikes.

Traditional Uses: Historically treated snakebites, colds, sore throats, toothaches, and earaches. Modern applications deploy it as a powerful cough syrup expectorant and throat gargle (acts as a laxative or emetic in high doses).

Makizin

Yellow Lady's Slipper

Cypripedium parviflorum

Habitat: Specialized woodland and bog environments, growing up to 31.25 inches with strongly ribbed leaves and a specialized pouch-shaped flower to enforce insect cross-pollination.

Traditional Uses: Critically revered in Ojibway legend (inspiring moccasin designs). Its unpleasantly scented roots function as an antispasmodic, sedative, and nerve tonic to treat severe anxiety, tension, and sleep issues. Requires alcohol tincture extraction.

Manomin

Wild Rice

Zizania aquatica

Habitat: Large aquatic grass growing 10 to 12 feet tall in shallow, slow-moving waters across eastern North America, ripening into dark purple seed-heavy spikes by autumn.

Traditional Uses: A vital regional dietary staple for First Nations. Seeds are boiled like rice, ground into meal for thickening soups and baking bread, providing a dense supply of essential riboflavin and niacin.

Mayagi Mashkiki

Hoary Puccoon

Lithospermum canescens

Habitat: Early-blooming prairie perennial strict to untouched virgin prairies spanning Saskatchewan to Texas. Grows 18 inches tall, wrapped in long white hairs with a central taproot.

Traditional Uses: Its distinctive ivory-like seeds and central taproot were historically utilized inside ceremonial rattles, and the plant was harvested to generate a rich, permanent reddish pigment.

Mayagi Mashkosiw

Big Bluestem

Andropogon gerardii

Habitat: Dominant prairie grass reaching 2 to 6 feet in height, featuring incredibly deep roots extending down to 12 feet to survive severe droughts. Favors fertile, dry lowland soils.

Traditional Uses: Forms dense clumps of long, flat leaves used historically in decoctions to manage high fevers and painful digestive tract disorders.

Mazaanaatig

Canada Thistle

Cirsium arvense

Habitat: Introduced by Europeans; thrives aggressively in disturbed lands, roadsides, and pastures. Highly branched, smooth-stemmed, standing 1 to 5 feet with spiny leaves.

Traditional Uses: Its deep roots have historically been prepared as astringents, diuretics, general tonics, and remedies for localized toothaches. The soft, fuzzy material on its seeds provides exceptional tinder for fire-starting.

Meskojiibikak

Bloodroot

Sanguinaria canadensis

Habitat: Low-growing perennial of eastern North American woodlands, recognizable by waxy-white early spring blossoms and a single deeply lobed grayish-green leaf.

Traditional Uses: Named for the vibrant red juice found in its rootstock. Used as a dye, a tool for divination, and a remedy for fevers/rheumatism. Modernly used as an expectorant for congestion. Contains dangerous, toxic opium-like alkaloids; avoid if pregnant.

Mitigomizh

Bur Oak

Quercus macrocarpa

Habitat: Massive, widely distributed tree exceeding 100 feet in height with deeply ridged bark and unique acorns half-covered by warty, long-fringed caps ripening in fall.

Traditional Uses: Exceptionally versatile: wood provided durable material for bows and spears, nut meat was processed into edible grits and pastes, and the inner bark was brewed into medicinal teas for chronic diarrhea and heart issues.

Namepine

Wild Ginger

Asarum canadense

Habitat: Low-growing ground cover in rich woods, featuring pairs of large, hairy, heart-shaped leaves that canopy a single ground-level reddish-brown flower.

Traditional Uses: Gives off a pungent ginger aroma; roots can be boiled with sugar into candy. Medicinally used to aid childbirth, ease whooping cough, heal wounds, and settle stomachs. Chewed by local sturgeon fishermen to prevent boats from flipping. Can cause skin irritation.

Niibaa'obag

Closed Gentian

Gentiana andrewsii

Habitat: Damp fields and open forest borders from Quebec to North Carolina. Grows 24 inches tall with tightly closed, cup-shaped blue-to-violet flowers.

Traditional Uses: The intensely bitter root has a long history of traditional use as an appetite stimulant, general tonic, laxative, and cathartic used to treat weak stomachs and historical nervous conditions.

Nookwezigan

Yarrow

Achillea millefolium

Habitat: Highly prevalent perennial in disturbed ditches and fields. Standing 1 to 3 feet tall with gray-green hairy stems and stalkless, fern-like fragrant leaves.

Traditional Uses: Crushed leaves release a distinct scent and stop bleeding instantly when applied directly to cuts. Steeped into a blood-purifying tea to treat fevers, colds, stomach cramps, headaches, and internal hemorrhaging.

Ode'iminidjiibik

Wild Strawberry

Fragaria virginiana

Habitat: Low-growing ground plant across North America, spreading via thin running stems, three-parted toothed leaves, and small five-petaled white spring flowers.

Traditional Uses: The sweet fruit holds immense cultural significance, marking the arrival of summer for women concluding traditional ten-day fasts to revitalize their bodies. Tea prepared from the leaves treats severe cholera and stimulates standard appetite.

Omakakiibag

Pale Touch-Me-Not / Jewelweed

Impatiens pallida

Habitat: Tall, translucent-stemmed plant of wet woods and shaded areas, featuring dangling pale yellow flowers and fragile seed capsules that burst violently when ripe.

Traditional Uses: The soothing liquid sap is applied externally as an immediate remedy for ringworm, warts, hemorrhoids, and chemical irritation from poison ivy or stinging nettles. Raw consumption is dangerous due to sharp calcium oxalate crystals (neutralized by cooking).

Oshkiniikwebagoons

Common Tansy

Tanacetum vulgare

Habitat: Erect, aromatic perennial growing 2 to 3 feet tall along disturbed roadsides and open fields, producing flat clusters of bright orange-yellow button flowers.

Traditional Uses: Historically used in small quantities for fevers, earaches, and abortions. Ingestion is now strongly discouraged because it contains tanacetum oil, which is highly poisonous and potentially fatal. Highly invasive; extracts make powerful organic pesticides.

Ozhaawashwa-mashkosiwan

Kentucky Bluegrass

Poa pratensis

Habitat: Densely clumped grass common across North American pastures and lawns, growing 1 to 3 feet tall with smooth stems, wide basal leaves, and pyramidal green spikelet clusters.

Traditional Uses: Heavily cultivated for continuous livestock grazing and lawn stabilization; noted as being remarkably difficult for an untrained eye to differentiate from related wild grass species.

Sasapkwanins

Wild Bergamot

Monarda fistulosa

Habitat: Fragrant, 2-to-4-foot-tall herbaceous perennial found in dry woods, thickets, and forest clearings east of the Rockies, producing two-lipped tubular lavender flowers.

Traditional Uses: Aromatic leaves are added in modest amounts to refresh salads and drinks, or dried and steeped into a potent, minty therapeutic tea valued as an insect repellent and a remedy for severe digestive complications and external burns.

Wabos'odji'bik

Wild Sarsaparilla

Aralia nudicaulis

Habitat: 19-inch-tall perennial of mixed and hardwood forests, featuring a stout woody stem splitting into a single leaf of 3-5 leaflets and a separate leafless stalk yielding dark summer berries.

Traditional Uses: Extremely popular 1800s spring tonic; settlers used berries for winemaking and the sweet rhizome to brew early root beer. Medicinally deployed to soothe chronic coughs, heal fresh cuts, treat ulcers, and manage painful shingles outbreaks.

Wezaawinagak

Canada Goldenrod

Solidago canadensis

Habitat: Tall, hairy-stemmed plant growing up to 5 feet high across North America, showcasing prominent veins on lanceolate leaves and tiny yellow flower clusters on arching branches.

Traditional Uses: Frequently falsely blamed for seasonal hay fever (the true culprit is Ragweed). Crushed roots or flower poultices were traditionally applied to alleviate painful boils, burns, and ulcers; leaves serve as an organic origin for rubber and latex extraction.

Wiigwaas

White Birch

Betula papyrifera

Habitat: Medium-sized hardwood tree with a signature white peeling trunk bark as it matures, thriving best in sandy, well-drained soils but tolerating bogs and gravelly terrains.

Traditional Uses: Incredibly versatile: sap is boiled into syrup or fermented into beer/wine; inner bark acts as emergency food; flexible wood crafted into bows/spears. Outer bark was fashioned into canoes, baskets, and cradles; resin acts as a disinfectant for stomach pain.

Wiinizikens

Panicled Aster

Symphyotrichum lanceolatum

Habitat: Tall plant (4 to 5 feet) producing a massive spread of white or slightly violet-tinged flower heads with pinkish-yellow centers from August to September in damp ditches.

Traditional Uses: Prepared as a healing infusion alongside other flora to reduce raging fevers, boiled into a decoction to wash open wounds, or dried into a powdered salve for skin abrasions. Inhaling smoke from burning blossoms cures stubborn nosebleeds.

Wiishkobanijiibik

Wild Licorice Root

Glycyrrhiza lepidota

Habitat: 1-to-3-foot-tall plant found in moist, sandy prairies and stream valleys, featuring sticky stems, opposite leaves, alfalfa-like cream flowers, and fruit covered in hooked spines.

Traditional Uses: Fleshy root contains glycyrrhizin (50x sweeter than sugar), tasting like sweet potato when slow-roasted. Roots and young spring shoots are edible raw or cooked. Historically deployed to treat deep skin sores, toothaches, foot ailments, and to safely aid childbirth.

Zesabiins

Indian Hemp

Apocynum cannabinum

Habitat: Sturdy, erect plant standing 3 to 4 feet tall with a purplish stem, opposite leaves with a white plum-like coating, and spindle-shaped seed pods in disturbed roadsides and lakeshores.

Traditional Uses: Incredibly strong fibers woven into durable twine and cordage that maintained strength underwater without shrinking—ideal for fishing nets, bowstrings, clothing, slings, and hunting nooses. **Contains dangerous cardio-toxins; must never be consumed.**
