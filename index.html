Historical Lifeways & Ethnobotanical Timeline


10,000 – 30,000 Years Ago | Ancient Migrations & Lifeways


After traveling across the ancient land bridge connecting Asia to North America, Indigenous groups developed unique plant-based care systems across Canada. Early records show that these communities lived incredibly healthy lives, with very few instances of bone or joint issues. Thanks to an active lifestyle, clean environment, and natural care practices, many individuals lived exceptionally long lives.

Pre-Contact Era | Holistic & Systemic Protocols


Traditional healing treated the mind, spirit, and body as connected pieces of overall health. Healers used healthy routines like fasting, cleansing vapor baths, and natural tonics to help restore physical balance. Long before modern sanitation frameworks were officially organized in Europe, Indigenous communities were already successfully tracking health patterns and keeping individuals with contagious illnesses safe and separate from others.

Post-Contact to Modern Era | The Bioprospecting Convergence


Over the last 500 years, early European settlers relied heavily on Indigenous plant expertise to survive, creating a shared history between traditional knowledge and native plants. Healers adapted their approaches—using plants like Devil's Club—to help combat newly introduced illnesses like smallpox and tuberculosis. This incredible understanding of plants directly laid the groundwork for modern medicine, leading to important breakthroughs like today's pain-relief treatments.



The Doctrine of Signatures


A fascinating idea in both historical European folk medicine and traditional Indigenous practice is the Doctrine of Signatures. This belief suggests that a plant's physical traits—like its shape, texture, environment, or juice colors—give natural clues about how it can help the body. Rather than just guessing, early practitioners used these visual clues as blueprints to discover which plants could address specific health needs.



🌱 Hepatica (Three-Lobed Leaf)


Because its unique three-part leaves look similar to the shape of a human liver, traditional healers and early settlers carefully chose it to help balance liver function and calm internal bleeding.

🩸 Bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis)


Named for the bright red juice inside its thick roots and stems, its resemblance to blood inspired Native American healers to utilize it as a supportive remedy for circulatory balance and managing fevers.
